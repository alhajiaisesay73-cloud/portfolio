Client build 01 / 04

Casa Locé

Live in production

A concierge that answers guest questions, recommends the right experience, and books the table. At any hour, without anybody on the other end.

They had an agent already. It was slow, it could not actually book, and it sent people to pages that did not exist. Experiences run from 45 dollars a head to 200, so a lost booking is not a lost lead. It is somewhere between 90 and 1,600 dollars that was already interested enough to start talking.

It knows who it is talking to

It recognises the guest before it says a word. A first-time visitor, a returning customer, somebody who has already booked, or somebody who is not a fit. A guest with a table already reserved gets a warm welcome, not another sales pitch.

It is not allowed to improvise

  • It knows what day it is. Ask for this Friday and you get this Friday, in the business's own time.
  • It only offers times that are genuinely free. Nobody is ever promised a table that does not exist.
  • It cannot book on a hunch. Every detail is checked again before a reservation is confirmed.
  • It does not fall over. If something unexpected comes back, the guest still gets a sensible reply.

Client build 02 / 04

Wellness Checkmate

Delivered, five stars

An Instagram DM system for a gut health brand, built around a Reel that was going viral while the automation behind it was quietly failing.

The comments were arriving. The flow that was supposed to answer them was not sending the video, which was the entire promise of the post. Everyone the Reel reached was landing in a conversation that went nowhere.

The bug was not where it looked

A video that will not send looks like a broken file or a platform limit. It was neither. The video only went out if the person tapped a particular button, and almost nobody did. So the automation ran to the end, reported success, and quietly sent nothing.

That is the failure mode worth paying someone to find. Nothing errors. Nothing alerts. The numbers look fine and the customer is gone.

What shipped

  • One message, changed in one place. Every reply comes from a single master version, so rewording it is one edit instead of fourteen.
  • New people and warm people go different ways. Strangers are invited into the free community first. The paid offer is made to people who already know him.
  • Ask if it is a bot and he answers. A short clip of him replying in his own voice, rather than a denial from a script.
  • Friends never get sold to. Personal contacts are fenced off, so a message from a mate never lands in a sales sequence.
  • He runs it now. Alerts arrive where his team already works, and the whole thing is written down so he is not dependent on me.

Client build 03 / 04

The Echo Journal

5 stars, became a retainer

An echocardiography education brand with 73,000 followers on Instagram and 48,000 on Facebook, and a newsletter the audience was not finding.

The catch nobody mentions up front

The tool his account runs on has no way of knowing whether somebody actually subscribed. So the obvious plan does not work. You cannot send the link, wait, and then chase only the people who ignored it, because nobody knows who ignored it.

I built the confirmation into the conversation instead. One tap to say they have joined, which takes them straight out of the follow-up. It relies on somebody tapping, so it is not perfect, and I told him that plainly. But it errs in the safe direction. A subscriber who skips the tap gets one more nudge, not a week of them.

Work out your own number

Take your follower count, and the share who would join your list if somebody asked properly at the moment they were already talking to you. Multiply by what a subscriber is worth to you over a year.

That is what an unbuilt funnel costs you annually, and it is the only number that decides whether this is worth doing.

Own build 04 / 04

Instagram DM Lead Qualification

My own system, not a client deployment

A system that carries somebody from a comment on a post to a booked call, and then shows you which comment produced which booking.

You can see which message became which call

Every new booking is matched back to the conversation that produced it. Not the campaign, not the month. The actual person, and the actual message they sent.

Most coaches can tell you how many DMs they got and how many calls they had. Very few can tell you which DM became which call. Attribution has to be built in at the first touch. It cannot be added later and backfilled.

The last follow-up says it is the last one

People who go quiet get four messages over a week, each one written for them from what they actually said. The last one admits that life gets busy, makes one sincere invitation, and says plainly that this is the final follow-up.

Most sequences just go silent. Telling someone the door is open and you are going to stop messaging is both more honest and more likely to get a reply.

My own operating system

I run my business on one of these too

Live, in daily use

Most people selling automation run their own business out of a notes app. I do not. Every account I own, across mail, calendar, files and chat, sits behind something I built, and I can ask one question instead of checking four places.

One question, every account

Off the shelf, an assistant reaches exactly one account. I have several, each with its own mail, calendar and files, and the work is spread across all of them. So I built something that reaches every one of them at once.

"Any client replies this week" searches all of them and comes back as a single answer. Chasing the same question through four inboxes is not a workflow, it is a tax.

Read by default, write on purpose

The chat side is the clearest example. It cannot send a message at all until I turn that on deliberately. That is not caution for its own sake. It means the worst a mistake can do is put a wrong answer on my screen, instead of a wrong message in somebody else's thread.

When someone asks me to build an agent that will touch their customers, this is the shape I argue for. Full autonomy right up to the point where a mistake would be embarrassing, and then a person.

The expensive part happens last

Everything cheap runs first. The sorting and the counting cost nothing, so they happen up front, and only what survives is worth paying the AI to look at. The AI is the last step, not the first.

That order is the whole discipline, and it is the same one I apply to a client build. It is why these systems stay cheap to run once they are live. Every unnecessary AI call is a bill you pay forever.

Written down, not improvised

The jobs I repeat are written down properly: what sets them off, what to check and in what order, what to do when something is missing or two sources disagree, and exactly what the answer should look like. A weekly review of my own setup scores it the same way every time.

A written standard is something I can argue with. A feeling is not. One of these runs every morning before the working day starts, and the rest run when I ask.

Why this is in a portfolio at all

Because it is the honest version of the pitch. A system I use every day, that I have had to live with the consequences of, tells you more about how I build than a demo that was assembled to be looked at once.

If you want the same thing pointed at your business instead of mine, that is a normal piece of work and it is one of the most common things I get asked for now.

The range

Six kinds of system, one way of building them

A conversation is only ever the front door. What makes a build worth paying for is the part behind it: the record that gets written, the calendar that gets checked, the person who gets told, and the trail that proves which enquiry turned into which customer.

01 AI agents

Agents that act, not just answer

An agent that can only talk is a brochure. Mine look the person up in the CRM, check real availability, write the booking, create the deal and hand off to a human when they are out of their depth.

02 CRM

CRM and pipeline automation

GoHighLevel and HubSpot. Stages that reflect how the business actually sells, lead source on every card, ownership and follow-up that fires without anyone remembering to set a reminder.

03 Booking

Booking and scheduling

Cal.com, Calendly and native CRM calendars. Real availability rather than invented slots, timezone handled properly, confirmation and reminders attached, and the booking landing in the CRM as revenue rather than as a calendar entry.

04 Lead systems

Lead capture, qualification and routing

Comments, DMs, forms and ads into one qualified queue. Scored, routed by what the person actually needs, and attributed from the first touch through to the booked call.

05 Outreach

Follow-up and outbound agents

Sequences written for the individual out of their own answers rather than merged into a template. Including knowing when to stop, and saying so, which is the part almost every sequence gets wrong.

06 Operations

Content and back-office operations

Newsletters, publishing pipelines, inbox triage, document generation and digests into Slack with a searchable archive behind them. The unglamorous work that quietly eats a day a week.

The through-line

What every one of these has in common

The interesting engineering is never the happy path. It is what the system does when something goes wrong, and whether anyone finds out.

01

State machines, not chatbots

Routing on a stored customer status, so a booked customer is never sold to again

02

The model is never trusted blindly

Structured output contracts, parsing that degrades instead of crashing, server-side checks before anything commits

03

Model choice is a cost decision

Sonnet for live conversation where quality is visible, Haiku for follow-ups where it is not

04

Monitoring ships with the build

A dedicated error workflow naming the exact failed node, with a link to that execution

05

Failure is isolated

One outside service having a bad day cannot strand a customer halfway through a booking

06

Attribution is built in on day one

The source travels from first touch to closed deal, because it cannot be reconstructed later

The tools are listed above and they matter less than the list suggests. What matters is whether the thing still works in three months, when nobody is watching it and the person who built it has moved on.
